# System 05 · Articles: Thinking

**Who it's for:** Thai M5/M6 students preparing for the TCAS70 A-Level English paper (14 March 2027), especially Reading Part V (items 45–60). These 16 items separate 60-point students from 80-point students, because more and more of them ask you to think beyond the words.

## The big idea

Hear the writer: what they mean, how they feel, how they build the text. One rule runs through every level: the answer is as strong as the evidence and no stronger.

## Level 1 · Beyond the words

**Inference: one small step.** An inference isn't written in the text, but the text makes it almost certain. The analogy: wet footprints from the sea to a towel mean someone has just been swimming. Anything more is a story you've made up.
- Example 1: a school moved its start time and "far fewer students were asking to lie down". Inference: before the change, many students felt tired in the mornings.
- Example 2: organisers "plan to hold the event every term", so they thought it worked.
- The trap: an option that's true about the world ("teenagers need more sleep") but that the passage never supports. The only evidence allowed is on the page.

**Expressions in context.** Take the expression apart, read one sentence either side, and check three things: good, bad or both; now or changed over time; picture or real meaning. The analogy: three skull emojis from a friend don't mean call an ambulance. Translate the feeling, not the picture.
- Example 1: TCAS69's "as entertaining as it has been disturbing" means both amusing and worrying, at the same time.
- Example 2: "Thailand punches above its weight" means it has far more influence than its size suggests.
- The trap: the time twist. "It used to be fun, but it has become dangerous" sounds like a paraphrase, but it adds a change the original never had.

**Conclusions.** A conclusion comes from the whole text, including its limits: "the study had limits", "only 240 students", "the evidence is mixed". The analogy: after two wins, the fan shouts, "We'll win the league!" and the coach says, "We've started well." Be the coach.
- Example 1: students who drafted before using AI wrote more original stories. Conclusion: when students use AI may matter. Not: "AI makes all writing less original."
- Example 2: moderate gamers did better on one test, heavy gamers worse on another: it depends.
- The trap: half a conclusion, one finding presented as the whole message.

## Level 2 · Voice

**Tone words.** Every tone word has a direction (positive, neutral, negative) and a strength (mild or strong). The analogy: a speaker's volume knob. "Critical" is a sad song at level 4, "hostile" is the same song at level 10.
- Example 1: a café review that complains about the noise but says "to be fair, the Wi-Fi was fast" is critical, not hostile.
- Example 2: "Congratulations… after a mere six years" about a failed bridge: nice words, bad facts, so sarcastic.
- The trap: the volume swap. The key and the near miss point the same way with different strength. One softener rules out the loudest word.

**The writer's attitude.** Attitude is the writer's position. Learn the confusing pairs: balanced (a fair judge who weighs both sides and reaches a view) vs ambivalent (torn, can't decide); critical vs hostile; concerned vs fearful; skeptical (doubts until there's evidence) vs dismissive. The analogy: at a bubble-tea shop, Fah compares and orders (balanced), while Mint loves two flavours and leaves with nothing (ambivalent).
- Example 1: "Critics call this proof that bans do not work. That conclusion may be premature." The writer weighs both sides and reaches a view: balanced.
- Example 2: "None of this is cause for panic. It is, however, a good reason to order spare filters now." Concerned, not fearful.
- The trap: choosing "balanced" just because two sides are mentioned. A writer who quotes critics and then says "That is unfair" has taken a side.

**Fact vs opinion.** A fact can be checked; an opinion is a judgement people could disagree with. Opinion signals: should, frankly, surely, alarming, unfair. The analogy: the scoreboard (fact), the commentator's shout (opinion), and the commentator saying "the coach called it their best game" (a fact about the coach).
- Example 1: "The authors recommend that employers stop sending messages after 9 p.m." is a fact, because it reports what they recommended.
- Example 2: "That advice is long overdue" is the writer's opinion.
- The trap: labelling reported opinions ("customers say they miss the old stalls") as opinions. Ask whose judgement it is.

## Level 3 · Architecture

**Organisation patterns.** There are seven blueprints: sequence, cause and effect, compare and contrast, problem and solution, classification, definition and example, and general to specific. Label each sentence with its job first, then use the signal words to confirm. The analogy: house plans. You're asked to name the floor plan, not the furniture.
- Example 1: TCAS69 tested a paragraph that made a general point and then gave a job interview as the case: general and specific.
- Example 2: a flood paragraph that explains a cause in one sentence and then lists three proposals is problem and solution.
- The trap: one "because" makes students choose cause and effect. The pattern is the shape of the whole paragraph.

**Why mention X?** Every detail has a job: exemplify, support, contrast, concede or warn. The claim it serves is in the sentence just before or after. The analogy: nobody asks why the goalkeeper is on the team "because she wears gloves". Name her position, not her kit.
- Example 1: a Thai singer eats som tam on a live stream and orders abroad stay high for weeks: an example of culture spreading when nobody pushes it.
- Example 2: "Admittedly, the extra minute made lessons slightly slower. But…" concedes a small weakness.
- The trap: the content echo, an option that repeats what X says without naming its job.

**What comes next, source and genre.** The last sentence is a promise: "the reasons for this are surprising" means reasons come next. Cross out options that go backwards, go sideways or contradict the text. For source questions, match the fingerprints: a headline means news, key terms mean a textbook, "Dear Parents" means a school letter. The analogy: a drama episode that ends on a ringing phone. Next week opens with who was calling.
- Example 1: "…the one surprising exception was the second claim", so the next paragraph explains what was found about that claim.
- Example 2: a school letter that mentions the Student Council was still written by the school.
- The trap: the backwards step, choosing a topic the passage has already finished.

## 60-second exam tactic

1. Name the job of the question: infer, expression, conclude, tone, attitude, fact/opinion, organisation, why mention, or next.
2. For inference and conclusions, point to the proof words. No proof, no answer.
3. For expressions and "why mention", read one sentence before and one sentence after.
4. For tone and attitude, find the direction, then the strength. Look for a softener.
5. Check the option's strength against the text's: all, always, prove versus may, can, depends.

## Quick-fire self-test

1. "Critical" or "hostile" for a review that says "to be fair, the staff were friendly"? **Critical.**
2. Is "The authors recommend eight hours of sleep" a fact or an opinion? **A fact, because it reports their recommendation.**
3. A writer feels pulled both ways and can't decide. Balanced or ambivalent? **Ambivalent.**
4. "Admittedly, the sample was small" does what job? **It concedes a weakness.**
5. A text has "Key terms" and a "Review question". Where is it from? **A textbook.**

## Pep talk

This is the thinking level of the paper, and it rewards careful readers, not fast ones. Satriwithaya students already debate and read between the lines in two languages. On exam day, do it on purpose: one small step, the same strength as the text, and a job for every detail. That's how 60 becomes 80.
