# System 04 · News & Articles: Understanding

**Who it's for:** Thai M5/M6 students preparing for the TCAS70 A-Level English paper (14 March 2027), especially Reading Part III (the news report, items 33–38) and Part V (the two long articles, items 45–60). That's 22 of the 80 items.

## The big idea

Each reading question asks you to do a particular job: find the whole point, find one exact fact, follow a cause to its effect, or keep each opinion with its owner. Once you know the job, you know where to look and what the trap will be.

## Level 1 · The big picture

**Main idea and best title: the Goldilocks test.** The main idea covers every paragraph and nothing more. Examiners build wrong answers in three sizes: too small (a true detail from one paragraph), too big (a general topic that fits any article), and off target (something twisted or never claimed). The analogy: a K-pop group photo. A close-up of one member is a detail; a shot of the whole stadium is too big; the main idea is the group photo with everyone in the frame.
- Example 1: an article about students who charged their phones in the kitchen. "Students slept 38 minutes longer" is true but too small. "Charging phones outside the bedroom helped teenagers sleep better" is just right.
- Example 2: TCAS69's Texas flood report. The key, "The floods raised questions about how authorities responded", paraphrased the headline's angle.
- The trap: choosing a true detail because you recognise it. Ask, "How many paragraphs does this cover?"

**Purpose.** Ask what the writer wants you to know, understand or do. Facts and sources mean inform; "because" chains mean explain; "you", "should" and commands mean persuade; "while" and "whereas" mean compare. The analogy: a Grab trip. The streets are the details; the purpose is the address typed in before the trip started.
- Example 1: a flood-safety column ending "So do it this weekend, not on the night the water arrives" is written to urge readers to prepare.
- Example 2: a passage listing Australia's under-16 ban, France's under-15 rule and the UK's plan for 2027 is written to compare.
- The trap: right verb, wrong object. Check both halves of "To offer advice on…".

**How news is built.** A news report is an upside-down pyramid: headline, then the lead with who, what, when, where and why, then facts, background, reactions, and finally the "what next?" questions. In TCAS, paragraph (1) is the headline, so paragraph (2) is usually the lead. The analogy: a 7-Eleven, where the things everyone wants are at the front.
- Example 1: "Millions of phones across Bangkok sounded an emergency alarm last week…" is a lead.
- Example 2: "Now the questions begin. Should alerts be sent earlier?" is the aftermath paragraph TCAS likes to ask about.
- The trap: right fact, wrong speaker. Check who said it: officials, residents or an expert.

## Level 2 · Finding it

**Detail and paraphrase.** The key usually says the same thing in different words. Distractors copy the passage's words and change one small thing: all for some, will for may, already for later. The analogy: a cover version of a song, with new instruments and the same melody.
- Example 1: "Not all farmers are convinced" becomes "Some farmers have doubts."
- Example 2: "Reservoirs have already run dry" is a trap, because the text said water will run low by the dry season.
- The trap: trusting the option that looks most like the passage.

**NOT / EXCEPT / FALSE: the tick method.** Three options are in the text. Circle the NOT, check every option, and mark it with a tick, a cross or a question mark. The one without a tick is the answer. A FALSE answer must contradict the text. The analogy: a teacher calling the register and waiting for "present".
- Example 1: "Teachers noticed all of the following EXCEPT…": a noisier canteen, more library books and fewer late students get ticks; "higher test marks" was never mentioned.
- Example 2: "Which is FALSE?": "The idea came from teachers" contradicts "it was the student council, not the teachers".
- The trap: forgetting the NOT halfway and choosing the first true option.

**Reference words.** It, this, those, such and both groups point back. List the candidates, check singular or plural, then substitute. The analogy: the "change here" arrow on a BTS Skytrain map.
- Example 1: "Both groups scored only slightly better than chance" means the university and secondary students, not the real and AI videos, because videos can't score.
- Example 2: "Apps have changed that": "that" is the whole idea that a video proves something happened.
- The trap: grabbing the nearest noun.

## Level 3 · Linking ideas

**Cause, effect and sequence.** Find the signal, draw the arrow, and answer about the link the question points to. "So", "as a result" and "lead to" point forward; "because", "due to" and "stem from" point back. "At the same time" is not a cause. The analogy: dominoes. Which domino actually hit this one?
- Example 1: why does smoke stay over Chiang Mai? Because the air is still, not because the Pacific is warm (that's domino one).
- Example 2: "Dry forests cause El Niño" reverses the arrow.
- The trap: a real cause from the wrong link in the chain.

**Who says what.** Sort the voices into boxes: supporters, critics, experts. The verb shows the strength: argue and insist are strong; admit and concede mean accepting a point against your own side. The analogy: fan clubs at a fan meeting, each in its own coloured block.
- Example 1: a mother campaigning for an under-16 rule wants "the law on our side", while a student group says "teach us to use it safely".
- Example 2: a researcher "admits" a link to poor sleep but "points out" that a link is not proof of cause.
- The trap: right opinion, wrong owner, or "linked" turned into "caused".

**Which detail best supports.** Most options are true, so the question is whether a detail proves the exact key word in the claim. The analogy: a lawyer needs the photo from Friday, not a real photo from Tuesday.
- Example 1: "The flooding affected the whole capital" is supported by "all 50 districts", not by "37 blocked roads".
- Example 2: "The rain was extremely intense" is supported by "over 300 mm in 48 hours", not by "22 deaths" (severity) or "water from the North" (cause).
- The trap: true but off target. Use the remove-it check.

## 60-second exam tactic

1. Read the headline and lead (or first and last paragraphs) and say the main idea to yourself in eight words.
2. Name each question's job: main idea, purpose, detail, NOT, reference, cause, views or support.
3. Jump to the right place: the lead for main ideas, the named person's paragraph for views, the pointer's sentence for reference.
4. Predict before you look at the options.
5. Distrust copied words; trust paraphrase.
6. On NOT/EXCEPT, tick every option.

## Quick-fire self-test

1. An option is true but only describes paragraph 3. Can it be the main idea? **No, it's too small.**
2. In a TCAS news report, which paragraph is usually the lead? **Paragraph (2); (1) is the headline.**
3. "Result in" or "result from": which points back to the cause? **Result from.**
4. What does "concede" tell you about a speaker? **They accept a point against their own side.**
5. In a FALSE question, is "not mentioned" enough? **No. The answer must contradict the text.**
