# TCAS70 Launchpad · System 02: Idioms, Sayings & Discourse Markers

**For:** M5/M6 students at Satriwithaya preparing for A-Level English (82) on 14 March 2027, from B1 to C1, aiming for 80+. This episode covers the conversation section (items 1–20), where TCAS69 put an idiom or a discourse marker in 11 of the 20 blanks.

## The big idea

In a TCAS dialogue, all four options are usually real, correct English idioms. Three of them are wrong **for this moment**. The examiners' favourite trick is the *family trap*: four idioms that share one word. When the options share a word, only the **job** decides: what is this speaker doing right now? So the rule for the whole system is: **name the job from the line before AND the line after the blank, then choose.** The line after is the most important clue on the paper.

## Level 1: Idioms & sayings

**Reaction idioms.** A reaction idiom is a move, like a sticker you send on LINE. *I'm all ears* invites someone to talk. *You can say that again* and *Tell me about it* agree strongly. *Count me in* joins a plan and *Count me out* refuses it. *My lips are sealed* promises to keep a secret.
Worked example 1: "I need to talk to someone." "Sure, ___ What's up?" The next line invites her to talk, so *I'm all ears*. *I'm up to my ears* means "I'm too busy", which rejects her.
Worked example 2: "Exams are the worst." "___ I cried over physics every week at your age." That is *Tell me about it*: strong agreement plus your own story. It does NOT mean "please explain".
The trap: choosing by the shared word. Translate every option into plain English first.

**Situation idioms.** These label a situation (learn them in families: colour, weather, food, sports): *play it by ear* (decide later, as things happen), *draw the line at* (set a limit), *out of the blue* (suddenly), *with flying colours* (excellently), *get cold feet* (last-minute nerves), *break the ice* (help people relax).
Worked example 1: "It depends on the rain, so let's ___ and decide on Saturday morning." That is *play it by ear*.
Worked example 2: "She studied every night for three months, so she passed ___." That is *with flying colours*, not *out of the blue*, which would mean the success was a surprise.
The trap: *out of the blue* (suddenly) versus *once in a blue moon* (very rarely).

**Proverbs.** A proverb sums up the *shape* of the whole story. Bad plus bad plus bad: *When it rains, it pours*. Bad turning good: *Every cloud has a silver lining*. Hard work then success: *No pain, no gain*. Promises versus actions: *Actions speak louder than words*.
Worked example 1: phone in the klong, no chicken rice, project left at home: *When it rains, it pours*.
Worked example 2: then the deadline is moved, giving extra time: now it's *Every cloud has a silver lining*.
The trap: the "picture" option. If the story has rain in it, *It's raining cats and dogs* looks tempting, but it only describes the weather, not the lesson.

## Level 2: Markers that steer

**Adding.** Same direction, but what kind of extra? Neutral: *on top of that*. Worse: *to make matters worse*. Predictable: *needless to say*. Surprising: *as a matter of fact*.
Worked example 1: "My leader is sick. ___ my laptop crashed." That is *To make matters worse*.
Worked example 2: "His new girlfriend is Japanese. ___ she lives in Tokyo, like you." That is *As a matter of fact*, a surprising, more specific fact.
The trap: *needless to say* only works if the listener could have guessed the next fact.

**Contrasting in talk.** First, the "but test": if "but" sounds wrong in the blank, every contrast marker is wrong. Then choose the kind of turn. Two sides: *on the other hand*. My own point plus an exception: *having said that*, *mind you*. A problem that doesn't change the plan: *even so*. Whatever happens: *at any rate*.
Worked example 1: "The bus leaves at seven or seven-thirty. ___ be at the gate by 6:45." That is *At any rate*.
Worked example 2: "He's lazy about forms. ___ he did win the science fair." That is *Mind you*, an afterthought that softens the criticism.
The trap: *mind you* doesn't cancel the first statement; it only limits it.

**Rephrasing and concluding.** Same idea, new words: *in other words*. Shrink to one line: *in a nutshell*. Verdict after weighing: *all things considered*. What matters most: *at the end of the day*.
Worked example 1: "Your paragraphs jump from topic to topic. ___ the reader gets lost." That is *In other words*.
Worked example 2: "Formulas matter. But ___ a tired brain can't use them." That is *at the end of the day*.
The trap: a rephrase never adds new information.

## Level 3: Markers that frame

**Opening and framing.** *Without further ado* starts the main event. *By the way* and *speaking of* open a side topic. *In that case* reacts to what the other person just said. *Now that* plus a clause gives a reason that is now true, while *as long as* gives a condition. Grammar decides half of these: *now that* and *as long as* join two clauses; *in that case* and *otherwise* can't.
Worked example 1: "___ the exams are finally over, we can train every evening." That is *Now that*.
Worked example 2: "Pun can only come on Tuesdays." "___ we'll move the matches to Tuesday." That is *In that case*.
The trap: *as long as* for something that is already a fact.

**Hedging and stance.** *As far as I know* means my knowledge is limited. *Apparently* means someone told me. *If I'm not mistaken* means "I think so, correct me". *To be honest* signals my real feeling. *As you can imagine* means you can guess how it felt.
Worked example 1: "___ yes, but check the class page to be sure." That is *As far as I know*.
Worked example 2: "The water came up to our knees at 2 a.m. ___ nobody slept much." That is *as you can imagine*.
The trap: *obviously* next to a sentence full of doubt.

**Lecture and argument talk.** *To make an analogy* (the next line says "like…"), *to make a distinction* (two things shown as different), *to spell it out* (say it so plainly nobody can miss it), *to go off on a tangent* (wander off the topic).
Worked example 1: "Greenhouse gases ___ work like a blanket on a hot night." That is *To make an analogy*.
Worked example 2: "I've hinted three times, so let me ___: one more missed session and you're off the team." That is *spell it out*.
The trap: in TCAS69, three meta-markers were distractors for a simple adding marker. Ask first whether the next line is a thinking move at all.

## 60-second exam tactic

1. Read the line AFTER the blank before you read the options.
2. Name the job in two words: "invite talk", "worse news", "react to news", "compare with like".
3. If the options share a word, translate each one into plain English.
4. Check grammar: does the blank need to join two clauses?
5. Choose, then re-read the whole exchange once with your answer in it.

## Quick-fire self-test

1. "Karaoke after the test?" "___!" (Count me in)
2. Three disasters in one morning: which saying? (When it rains, it pours)
3. "___ the rain has stopped, let's walk." (Now that)
4. "Nobody told me directly. ___ the trip is cancelled." (Apparently)
5. A lecturer says: "___ memory is like a muscle: use it or lose it." Which marker? (To make an analogy)

## Pep talk

These blanks are not luck. Ask *what is this speaker doing right now?*, trust the line after the blank, and the conversation section becomes the quickest 25 points on the paper. Fighting, M5!
