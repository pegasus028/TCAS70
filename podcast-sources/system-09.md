# System 09 · Word Forms & Word Order

**For:** M5/M6 students at Satriwithaya preparing for A-Level English (82) on 14 March 2027, aiming for 80+. Level B2–C1. You already know the words. This episode is about putting the right form in the right seat, fast.

## The big idea
Word forms and word order are the biggest grammar family in Text Completion (items 61–75): about 13 of the 60 items across TCAS66–69. The options are often four forms of one word, like *efficient / efficiency / effective / effectively*. Meaning can't separate them, but position can. The core technique is **slot analysis**: cover the options, read three words on each side of the gap, and name the job (noun, adjective, adverb or verb). Then delete every option that doesn't fit.

## Level 1 · Forms

**Slot analysis.** *the / its / adjective* + ___ + *of* → **noun**. *a / the* + ___ + noun → **adjective**. ___ + adjective, or a word describing a verb → **adverb**. *can / to* + ___ → **base verb**. If two options survive, choose by meaning. *Analogy:* Tetris. The four options are one block turned four ways, so look at the hole first. *Worked examples:* "Long-term ___ to smoke damages the lungs" needs a noun: **exposure**. "Stress can ___ affect sleep" needs an adverb: **negatively**. *Trap:* the word next to the gap isn't always its partner. In "recalled the lesson ___ after a late night", the gap describes *recalled*, so it needs an adverb. Draw an arrow to the partner.

**Word families and suffixes.** The suffix tells you the job. *-tion, -ment, -ness* and *-ity* make nouns. *-al, -ous, -ive* and *-able* make adjectives. *-ise, -ify* and *-en* make verbs, and *-ly* usually makes an adverb. Prefixes like *un-, in-* and *dis-* change the meaning, not the job. *Analogy:* a family party. Everyone shares a surname (the root), and each wears a job name tag. When two cousins wear the same tag, ask what each one means. *Worked examples:* "Parents doubted the ___ of the app" needs a noun, and the meaning that fits is **reliability**, not *reliance*. "The results were ___: some classes improved, others didn't" needs **inconsistent**. *Trap:* picking the option that looks more advanced. "Language is part of who you are" needs **identity**, not *identification*. And *costly* and *friendly* are adjectives.

**Confusables.** One question splits each pair. *Affect* is the verb (A for Action) and *effect* is the noun (E for End result). *Rise* takes no object, but *raise* needs one, so ask "raise what?" *Likely to* means "probably will". *Analogy:* a balloon rises by itself, but a lift raises its passengers. *Worked examples:* "Caffeine can ___ your focus" → **seriously affect**. "The canteen ___ its prices" has an object → **raised**. *Trap:* mixed halves like *negatively effect* and *negative affect*. Decide verb or noun first, then match: an adverb goes with *affect*, an adjective with *effect*.

## Level 2 · Phrases

**Noun phrases and compounds.** The head noun comes **last**. Describers in front of it never take *-s*: *a ten-minute walk*, *a seven-year-old girl*. A plural can only go on the head, as in *seven-year-olds*. *Analogy:* a Skytrain. The engine (the head) carries the plural sign and the carriages never do. *Worked examples:* "rising levels of smartphone ___" needs a head noun: **addiction**. "The tour includes a ___ boat ride" → **two-hour**. *Trap:* your "plural brain" writes *two-years-old*. Ask which word is the head.

**Adjective order.** The order is opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose, then the noun. *Handmade* usually comes just before origin. *Analogy:* a concert. The emotional fans (opinion) scream from the back, while the stage crew (material) and the band's staff (purpose) stand beside the stars. *Worked examples:* **a lovely long red Thai silk scarf**; **a huge round white plastic water tank**. *Trap:* putting the "important" word first, as in *a Thai beautiful scarf*. Label each word and check the first two.

**Adverb placement and negation.** Short adverbs like *simply, even* and *probably* go after the first auxiliary and before the main verb. They never go between a verb and its object, so say *speak English fluently*. *Enough* comes after an adjective (*strong enough*). *Analogy:* a reserved Skytrain seat right behind the driver (the auxiliary). *Worked examples:* "Learning Thai ___ a school subject but a bridge to her grandparents." The *but* demands **is not simply**. "Most users ___ notice the update" → **will probably not**. *Trap:* *simply is not* is grammatical but means "definitely isn't". Read to the end.

## Level 3 · Tricky pairs

***-ed* / *-ing* and linking verbs.** The *-ed* form describes the feeler, and the *-ing* form describes the cause. After linking verbs like *be, seem, look* and *remain*, use an adjective. After action verbs, use an adverb. *Analogy:* TikTok. The clip is *amazing* and the viewer is *amazed*. *Worked examples:* "Parents were ___ by the screen-time figures" → **alarmed**. "During the debate she remained ___" → **calm**. *Trap:* adding *-ly* to whatever follows *look*. Use the *is*-test: "she looks calm" works as "she is calm", so it takes an adjective. But *looked calmly at him* is an action.

**Comparatives in context.** Choose the seat first, then add *more* or *less*. Use an adverb for a verb and an adjective after a linking verb. "The more…, the more…" needs *the* in both halves. Use *fewer* with countable nouns and *less* with uncountable ones. Never use *more* together with *-er*. *Analogy:* upgrading game gear. The seat decides the item, then you add the level. *Worked examples:* "Students solve problems ___ after a sleepless night" → **less efficiently**. "The longer you wait, ___ it becomes" → **the harder**. *Trap:* *less effect / less effective / less effectively / less effectiveness*. Cover *less*, name the seat, then put *less* back.

**Fronting and inversion (C1 stretch).** After a negative or limiting word at the front (*Rarely, Never, Not only, Little, No sooner*), switch to question order. If there's no auxiliary, add *do/does/did*. With *Only when* and *Not until*, invert the main clause. *Analogy:* a VIP arrives at Siam Paragon, and security (the auxiliary) jumps in front of the crowd (the subject). *Worked examples:* "Seldom ___ such long queues for a concert" → **have fans formed**. "Only after the results came out ___ how close the vote was" → **did we realise**. *Trap:* statement order looks right because it is right in a normal sentence. When a trigger opens the sentence, look for the auxiliary before the subject.

## 60-second exam tactic
1. Cover the options. Read three words on each side and name the seat: N, Adj, Adv or V.
2. Find the gap's real partner, even if it's three words away.
3. Kill every option with the wrong part of speech. If two survive, decide by meaning.
4. For order items, label each word and check the first two.
5. If the sentence opens with *Rarely / Not only / Only when*, look for auxiliary + subject.

## Quick-fire self-test
1. "The ___ of the bridge took two years." (construct / construction / constructive) → **construction**
2. "Prices have ___ sharply." (raised / risen) → **risen**, because there is no object.
3. "a ___ bag" (black old ugly / ugly old black) → **ugly old black**
4. "The lecture was so ___ that Pun fell asleep." (bored / boring) → **boring**, because the lecture causes the feeling.
5. "Never ___ so many students apply." (the school has seen / has the school seen) → **has the school seen**

## Pep talk
When four options look almost the same, that's good news. Meaning is off the table, so only position decides, and you can read position. Name the seat, find the partner, check the order, and these thirteen-odd items become your fastest points on the paper. You've got this, Satriwithaya.
