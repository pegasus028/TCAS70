# System 03 · Ads & Reviews — the twelve marks you can win on speed

**Who it's for:** M5/M6 students at Satriwithaya preparing for the A-Level English (82) paper on 14 March 2027. This episode covers Reading Part I (Advertisements, items 21–26) and Part II (Product/Service Review, items 27–32). Twelve items, fifteen points — and they reward method more than vocabulary.

## The big idea

Ads and reviews are persuasive texts, and that makes them predictable. An ad wants one kind of reader to do one thing. A review gives a verdict and backs it up with evidence. Once you know the machine, you know where every answer lives. The target: under a minute to read an ad, around three minutes for a review, and no re-reading.

## Level 1 · Advertisements

**Purpose and audience.** Read every ad in four stops: headline → offer → conditions → contact. The headline tells you who the ad is talking to ("Backpacking on a student budget?"). The call to action at the bottom tells you the purpose in one verb: book, apply, download, visit. A quick money test helps: if the reader pays, the ad is selling; if the reader gets paid, it is recruiting. TCAS67 used an au pair ad, and the answer was simply "caretakers". The ad was hiring people, not selling a service.
*Analogy:* a TikTok clip. The first two seconds hook a certain viewer, and the last frame says "follow for part 2". Hook = audience. Final tap = purpose.
*The trap:* the "too narrow" option. One real bullet point gets dressed up as the purpose of the whole ad. The real purpose has to explain the headline AND the call to action.

**Details and "NOT mentioned".** Don't hunt for the answer. Take attendance instead: for each option, find its "home" line in the ad and tick it. Paraphrases count, so "Promote Blood Circulation" means the same as "promotes better blood flow". The option with no home is the key. Keep the two question types apart: NOT mentioned means absent, NOT true means the ad says the opposite.
*Worked examples:* (1) A sun-milk ad gives the price, the water resistance and "tested on sensitive skin", but not where it is made, so that is NOT provided. (2) The headline promises "protection for the whole school day", but the fine print says "reapply every 2 hours", so "one layer lasts all day" is NOT true.
*The trap:* world knowledge. Every scooter has a top speed, but if the ad doesn't say it, it isn't mentioned.

**Persuasion techniques.** Name the technique by what the words do to you. "Only 50 places" makes you hurry (urgency or scarcity). "Join 1.2 million users" says everyone is doing it (bandwagon). "Dr" or "certified" asks you to trust an expert. A named customer's quotation is a testimonial. Measurable facts about the product are a logical reason. Memories and pride are an emotional appeal. "Escape", "quiet" and "breathe" appeal to a desire for peace and relaxation. In TCAS69, "Escape the rush" came with two emotional-sounding options, and the more specific one, peace and relaxation, was right.
*The trap:* numbers. A number of people is bandwagon ("3,000 members"). A number about the product is a logical reason ("blocks 95% of noise").

## Level 2 · Reviews

**The overall verdict.** Reviews are sandwiches: a hint in paragraph 1, evidence in the middle, and the verdict at the end. Read the first and last paragraphs first. Label the verdict positive, mixed, or negative, then check its strength. In TCAS69, the food-delivery reviewer "works well overall but still clearly needs improvement". An option calling it "completely unsatisfactory" has the right direction but the wrong strength.
*Analogy:* a judge on a singing show can talk for a minute about one shaky note, but what counts is the score she holds up at the end.
*Worked examples:* (1) "My only complaint is the stairs … I would book again without thinking twice" is highly positive. (2) "One great cake cannot save a café … I won't be going back" is negative, even though the cake got praise.
*The trap:* a vivid complaint in paragraph 4 pulls you towards a harsher answer than the conclusion supports.

**Strengths, drawbacks and proof.** Every claim comes with a receipt. "The trainers are another plus" is proved by "a trainer wrote me a four-week plan at no extra cost". A true detail from a different paragraph doesn't support the claim. Read frequency words like a lawyer: in TCAS69, the missing drinks happened "on two occasions", but the long wait happened once.
*The trap:* the true-but-irrelevant option. Ask "Does this prove THIS claim?", not "Is this in the text?"

**Reading between the lines.** An inference is a small, safe step. Clues include the company's promise in quotation marks next to reality ("feedback within 48 hours" that arrived after six days), hedges like "just about" and "perhaps", and what the reviewer actually did ("I politely said no"). If an option says "never", "always" or "definitely", it has usually gone too far.
*Analogy:* your friend's LINE reply, "The cake was nice 🙂". You know at once the rest was bad.

## Level 3 · Two texts and fine print

**Ad A vs Ad B.** Build a mini table with the same four rows for both ads: offer, price, conditions, contact. BOTH means the feature is in A and in B, even in different words ("4 mock exams" and "10 mock exams"). UNLIKE means it's in only one. For "which ad suits this person, and why", turn the person into one need and check both halves of the option. Right ad plus wrong reason is still wrong.

**Fine print.** Think of it as a row of BTS ticket gates: WHO (age, students only, valid ID), WHEN (offer ends, date range, while stocks last), HOW MANY (one per customer, minimum three nights), HOW (in stores only, direct bookings only, not valid with other promotions). You have to pass every gate. A warranty covers manufacturing faults only, not dropping, swimming or losing the product. A refundable deposit comes back to you; a non-refundable fee never does.
*Worked example:* a student with a valid ID who also wants to use trade-in credit loses the student price, because the fine print says the offer "cannot be combined with trade-in credit".

**Consumer vocabulary.** Learn these words in families. Free: complimentary, waived, at no extra cost. Pay: subscription, deposit, renews automatically. Rules: eligible, valid, redeem, non-transferable. Quality: versatile, durable, amenities. Old and new: predecessor, successor. The favourite trap is *complimentary*: it means free, not "full of praise".

## 60-second exam tactic

1. Ads: headline → offer → conditions → contact, then go to the questions. 2. NOT items: tick three homes and choose the leftover. 3. Technique items: quote the exact words and pick the most specific label. 4. Reviews: read the last paragraph before the attitude question. 5. Fine print: walk each person through every gate. 6. Two ads: four rows, then a row lookup for each question.

## Quick-fire self-test

1. "Only 20 seats left!" Which technique? *Scarcity/urgency.*
2. "Join 50,000 students already using it." Bandwagon or logical reason? *Bandwagon, because it counts people.*
3. A review ends "If you train in the morning, it's excellent value; if not, be ready to wait." Positive, mixed or negative? *Mixed/conditional.*
4. What does "predecessor" mean? *The earlier model, the one that came before.*
5. The warranty "covers manufacturing faults only". Is a cracked case from a fall covered? *No. That's damage the owner caused.*

## Pep talk

Ads and reviews are the friendliest texts in the paper, and they're written to be understood in seconds. Every minute you save on items 21–32 is a minute you can spend on the long articles later. So read like a shopper who knows the tricks: calm, quick, and hard to fool. See you in System 04.
