# TCAS70 Launchpad — System 12: Linkers, Determiners & Parallelism

**Who this is for:** M5 and M6 students at Satriwithaya getting ready for the A-Level English (82) paper on 14 March 2027, and especially for Text Completion (items 61–75). Out of the 60 Text Completion items in TCAS66–69, about 14 test the small words: connectors (8), determiners and pronouns (3) and parallel structure (3). That is roughly 17 points you can win with a method instead of a feeling.

## The big idea

Small words carry the logic. A linker says how two ideas relate. A determiner says how many there are and which ones we mean. A coordinator or a correlative pair keeps both sides of a sentence balanced. What decides each blank is one check students often skip: **look at what comes after the blank.**

## Level 1 — Linkers: the double check

**The rule.** Every linker has to pass two checks. First, meaning: is the second idea a contrast, a surprise, a cause, a result, an extra point or an example? Second, grammar: what does the linker plug into? There are three kinds of socket. *Although, while, whereas, because* take a full clause (subject + verb). *Despite, because of, owing to, unlike, in addition to, as well as* are prepositions, so they take a noun or an -ing form. *However, therefore, moreover, on the other hand* begin a new sentence, or come after a semicolon, and have a comma after them.

**Analogy.** A travel adaptor. Meaning is the voltage and grammar is the shape of the plug. *Despite* has a noun-shaped plug, and it will never fit a clause-shaped socket, however hard you push.

**Contrast and concession — worked examples.**
1. "___ the rule is unpopular, classrooms are calmer." A clause follows, and the second idea is a surprise, so the answer is *Although*. *Despite* has the right meaning but the wrong socket.
2. "Some students feel anxious, ___ others talk more at lunch." Two groups are placed side by side and a clause follows, so the answer is *whereas* or *while*, not *unlike* and not *however*.
**Trap:** "despite + subject + verb". Underline the first verb after the blank before you look at the options.

**Cause, result and purpose — the arrow.** Draw cause → result. *Because* and *owing to* go in front of the cause. *So* and *therefore* go in front of the result. *So that* and *in order to* go in front of the aim.
1. "___ the heavy rain, traffic was blocked at 37 locations." The rain is the cause and a noun follows, so *Owing to*.
2. "Warnings were sent ___ residents could move their cars." This is an aim, and a subject + *could* follows, so *so that*. *In order to* would need a bare verb.
**Trap:** *because* and *therefore* are in the same family but point in opposite directions.

**Addition, example and source.** *As well as* and *in addition to* take a noun or -ing (*it weakens memory, as well as raising the risk…*). *Moreover,* and *In addition,* start a new sentence. *Such as* and *like* introduce examples. *According to* names a source, not a cause. When all four options take a noun (*Owing to / Contrary to / According to / In addition to*), ask what the noun *is*: a source, a cause, a belief being corrected, or an extra item?

## Level 2 — Determiners: count, then point

**The "other" grid.** Look at the noun first. *Another* + singular noun (one more), or + number + plural (*another six months*). *Other* + plural noun (*other countries*). *The other* is the second of a known pair, and *the others* are the rest of a set. *Others* stands alone and is never followed by a noun. *Each other* describes a two-way action, and its possessive is *each other's homes*.
**Analogy:** a binder of photocards. Another card is any one more, the other card completes your pair, and the others are the rest of your set.
**Worked example:** "Some visitors joined the dance club; ___ preferred robotics." No noun follows, so *others*. "___ countries soon followed." A plural noun follows, so *Other*.
**Trap:** choosing an -s form in front of a plural noun. *Others countries* is always wrong.

**Quantifiers.** *Every, each, either* and *neither* go with a singular noun and a singular verb. *Both* means exactly two, together (plural). *All, most* and *some* go with a plural or uncountable noun. On its own, *Some* can mean "some people": "*Some argue* that such rules are unrealistic."
**Worked example:** "However, ___ group was willing to give up phones: parents needed them for work, teenagers for homework." Both *either* and *neither* fit the grammar, but the reasons make the meaning negative, so *neither*.
**Trap:** *either* versus *neither*. Read the next line to decide whether the meaning is positive or negative.

**Pronoun reference.** Draw an arrow back to the real noun. One organisation is *it/its*: "A group of twelve schools *has* changed *its* rules." The verb *has* proves the subject is singular. People take *their*, and so does one person of unknown gender: "each student… *their* name". Generic *one* goes with *one's*. In comparisons, use *that of* for singular or uncountable nouns and *those of* for plural ones: "the sugar in bubble tea is double *that* in cola".
**Trap:** matching the pronoun to the nearest noun instead of the real subject.

## Level 3 — Balance

**Parallel structure.** Whatever is joined by *and, or, but* or *than* has to share one shape. "They won by collecting uniforms, repairing fans and *opening* a swap shop." "Repairing is easier than *buying*." Find the first item in the list, because that is what sets the shape.
**Analogy:** a K-pop line dance. One member doing a different move ruins the formation.

**Correlatives.** The pairs are fixed: *both… and, either… or, neither… nor, not only… but also*. Look to the right of the blank for the partner word. If you see *nor*, the answer is *neither*. If you see *but also*, the answer is *not only*. Then balance what comes after each half: "not only *to win* trophies but also *to learn*." With *or* or *nor*, the verb agrees with the nearer subject: "Neither the students nor their teacher *was* told."

**FANBOYS.** *For, and, nor, but, or, yet, so.* A comma plus one of these joins two full sentences, and a comma on its own never can. *For* means "because". *So* gives a result. *Yet* is a surprising "but", and it can open a sentence. *Nor* needs question order after it: "They did not panic, *nor did they* rush to leave."
**Worked example:** "They had lived through floods many times, ___ they trusted their experience." The second idea is a result, so *so*, not *for*.
**Trap:** mixing up *so* and *for*. Ask whether the second clause is the reason or the result.

## 60-second exam tactic

1. Cover the options.
2. Read what comes after the blank: a clause, a noun or -ing, a new sentence, a singular or plural noun, or a partner word such as *nor* or *but also*.
3. Name the relation or the number in one word.
4. Uncover the options. Grammar usually removes two of them and meaning removes the third.
5. Check the next line; TCAS often hides the proof there.

## Quick-fire self-test

1. "___ the traffic, we arrived on time." → *Despite* (a surprise, and a noun follows)
2. "She stayed for ___ three weeks." → *another* (number + plural)
3. "The app has changed ___ privacy settings." → *its*
4. "Neither the coach nor the players ___ ready." → *were* (the nearer subject is *players*)
5. "It was raining, ___ we cancelled the picnic." → *so* (result)

## Pep talk

Small words, reliable points. Look right of the blank every time: double check, count the noun, match the shapes, move on.
