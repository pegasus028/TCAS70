# System 10 · Clause Architecture

**For:** M5/M6 students at Satriwithaya preparing for A-Level English (82) on 14 March 2027, aiming for 80+. Level B2–C1. You already write participle clauses in your essays; this episode turns that skill into exam points.

## The big idea
About one in six Text Completion items (61–75) in TCAS66–69 tested how clauses fit together: relative clauses, shrunken clauses and noun clauses. One habit solves most of them: **count the verbs**. Every clause gets exactly one finite verb, meaning a verb with tense, like *plays*, *is* or *has caused*. If the sentence already has its main verb, the blank must be a relative clause or a non-finite form (-ing / -ed). If the main verb is missing, the blank has to supply it. TCAS67 opened with exactly this: *"Soft power, ___ as the ability to influence others…, ___ a vital role."* Blank 62 is the main verb (*plays*), so blank 61 can't be one. The answer is *which is often described*.

## Level 1 · Relative clauses

**Who, which, that, whose, where.** A relative word stands for a noun and does that noun's job inside its own clause. Use *who* for people and *which* for things, *that* for either in defining clauses, and *whose* before a noun that is owned. Use *where* only when the clause is already complete. *Analogy:* the relative word is an interchange station like Siam, sitting on two lines at once. *Worked examples:* "the cultural threads ___ generations" (TCAS69 pattern). The sentence already has *is*, and threads do the connecting, so the answer is **that connect**, not *connect* or *that are connected*. Second example: "a canal ___ their adviser grew up". *Grew up* is complete, so the answer is **where**. *Trap:* reading locally ("threads connect generations") and choosing the bare verb. Underline the main verb first.

**Non-defining clauses and sentence-level which.** Commas mean the clause is extra, like brackets, so you can use *which, who* or *whose*, but never *that*. After a comma, *which* can point back to a whole idea: "4.7 million accounts were removed, **which** sounded like a clear success." *Analogy:* a fan caption under a photo of an idol everyone already knows. *Worked example:* the TCAS68 dress-code item. The options were *that has caused / that it has caused / which has caused / which it has caused*. The comma rules out *that*, and *which it* gives the verb two subjects, so the answer is **which has caused**. *Trap:* comma + *it* ("…, it has caused debates") is a comma splice.

**Preposition + which/whom.** Rebuild the clause as a plain sentence to find the preposition: men and women communicate *through* body language, so "a factor **through which** they communicate" (TCAS66). The same test gives "a skill **of which** they are proud" and "the extent **to which** they switched". After a preposition, only *which* and *whom* are allowed. Use "400 students, **most of whom** speak Thai", not *most of them*. *Analogy:* the preposition is carry-on luggage, allowed in the front seat or at the back but never both. *Trap:* the double preposition, as in "with which they fall in love with".

## Level 2 · Shrinking clauses

**Reduced relatives.** Delete *who/which + be* and only the verb's costume is left. If the noun **does** the action, use -ing ("pieces of plastic **measuring** less than 5 mm"). If it **receives** the action, use -ed ("particles **released** by tyres", "a study **published** in 2026"). *Analogy:* a shrink ray. It makes the clause smaller but never changes who does what. *Worked example:* "Songkran, ___ as the world's biggest water fight, has become…". *Has become* is the main verb, and Songkran is described by people, so the answer is **which is described**. *Trap:* treating -ed as "past". Here it means passive, not past.

**Appositives.** An appositive is a noun phrase that renames the noun next to it: "Dr Nattaya Srisuk, **a psychologist**, says…". It has no verb of its own. TCAS69 used "IQ scores rose steadily, ___ as the Flynn Effect" with options like *a trend is known*. Any option with *is* adds a second main verb, so it's out. *Analogy:* a name tag at a fan meet. It names the idol, but it never talks. *Trap:* choosing *is a psychologist* because it "sounds complete". In the middle of a sentence, you need a comma on both sides.

**Reduced adverbial clauses.** Keep the conjunction and drop the subject + *be*: "when **experienced** in moderation" (TCAS67), "if not **prepared correctly**" (TCAS68), "while **revising**". The hidden subject must be the main clause's subject. *Worked examples:* "Caffeine can sharpen attention when ___ in small amounts". Caffeine is consumed, so the answer is **consumed**. "___ correctly, the system can reach millions". The system is used, so the answer is **If used**. *Analogy:* a Grab rider travelling light. She leaves the subject at home but always keeps the address pin (the conjunction). *Trap:* the dangling modifier. "When cooked too long, Pun's mother says the noodles go soft" means his mother got cooked.

## Level 3 · Noun clauses

**Embedded questions.** A question placed inside a sentence becomes a noun and takes statement order, with no *do/does/did*: "interest in **what the other person says**" (TCAS66), "the question of **how nations use** soft power" (TCAS67), "**how creativity and technology can lead to**" (TCAS68). *Analogy:* fold the paper plane's wings flat so it fits in the envelope. *Worked example:* "check ___ before sharing" → **what the account has posted before**, not *what has the account posted*. *Trap:* copying the question you hear in your head.

**Whether vs if.** After *ask, know* or *wonder*, you can use either. At the start of a sentence, after a preposition, before *to* and right before *or not*, only **whether** works. "**Whether** the ban has worked is still unclear." "The debate is about **whether** to act." TCAS69 also used *Whether or not* to mean "it doesn't matter if". *Analogy:* whether has a VIP wristband that gets it into every zone, while *if* has a general ticket. *Trap:* choosing *If* at the start because it feels like a conditional.

**It + passive/adjective + that/to.** Dummy *It* saves the subject seat for a heavy clause at the end. After reporting verbs, use the passive: "It **has been established** that teenagers need eight to ten hours." After adjectives, use *to*: "It is essential **to maintain** a balance" (TCAS67). You can also use *for + person + to*, or *that* + a full clause. The twin structure is "The app **is said to have reduced** screen time." *Analogy:* the bag you drop on a canteen seat while you queue. *Trap:* *that maintains*, which has no subject after *that*.

## 60-second exam tactic
1. Before reading the options, find the main verb of the sentence. Is it there or missing?
2. Kill options that give the sentence two main verbs or none.
3. If there's a comma before the blank, kill *that*. If there's a noun after the blank, think *whose*.
4. For -ing vs -ed, put the noun back as the subject and ask: does it act, or is it acted on?
5. For wh-clauses, use statement order and no *do*. At the start of a sentence or after a preposition, use *whether*, not *if*.

## Quick-fire self-test
1. "The rules ___ last year ban phones in class." (introduced / introducing) → **introduced**, because the rules were introduced.
2. "Body language is a factor ___ men and women communicate." → **through which**
3. "Students revise late, ___ explains their tiredness." (that / which / it) → **which**
4. "The question of ___ soft power" (how do nations use / how nations use) → **how nations use**
5. "___ AI helps students is still debated." (If / Whether) → **Whether**

## Pep talk
Near-identical options are good news: only grammar can separate them, and that grammar is small. Count the verbs, check the commas, ask who does what — and these become your fastest points. You've got this, Satriwithaya.
