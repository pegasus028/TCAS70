# System 11 · Verb Systems

**For:** M5/M6 students at Satriwithaya preparing for A-Level English (82) on 14 March 2027, aiming for 80+. Level B2–C1.

## The big idea
In Text Completion (items 61–75), when all four options are forms of one verb (*expand / expanding / is expanded / has expanded*), the examiner is testing the verb system. Each one yields to four questions, asked in order: **What is the time anchor? What is the true head noun of the subject? Does the subject do the action or receive it? What does the word before the gap demand?**

## Level 1 · Time

**Tense from time signals.** Thai marks time with words (*แล้ว, จะ*); English makes the time word and the verb agree. A finished time (*in 2025, last month, ago*) takes the past simple. A period that reaches now (*since, since then, so far, recently, for … now*) takes the present perfect. After *when, by the time, as soon as* about the future, use the present simple. *Analogy:* the Grab app says "Driver arrived at 8:05" (time stamp, past simple) and "Your driver has arrived" (result now, present perfect). *Worked examples:* "In December 2025, Australia ___ the first country to ban under-16 accounts" → *became*, not "has become". "Since then, other governments ___ similar rules" → *have announced*. *Trap:* the anchor is often a sentence away.

**Agreement with long subjects.** The verb agrees with the head noun, not the noun nearest the gap. Bracket every *of…, for…, on…, who…* phrase until one noun is left. TCAS67: "pressure on students for good grades ___ change" → *is prompting*. *Analogy:* at a fan meeting the fans are loud and close to the microphone, but the interviewer only questions the idol. *Worked examples:* "The number of students who use chatbots ___ risen" → *has*. "Each of the new guidelines ___ an example" → *includes*. *Trap:* a plural noun such as *grades* or *students* sits right before the gap.

**Perfect and continuous aspects.** Perfect looks back from a reference point: now (*have*), a past moment (*had*) or a future deadline (*will have*). Perfect continuous is an activity running up to that point. TCAS68: "Scientists ___ on developing this toilet for several years" → *have been working*. *Analogy:* a download bar. "Has downloaded three episodes" is a count; "has been downloading for an hour" is the moving bar. *Worked examples:* "By the time the rain stopped, volunteers ___ sandbags for three days" → *had been filling*. "By the end of the year, members ___ 2,000 hours" → *will have completed*, because a counted amount takes the simple perfect. *Trap:* seeing *for* and grabbing *have been + -ing* when the reference point is past or future. State verbs (*know*) never take -ing.

## Level 2 · Voice

**Passive in all tenses.** Ask who does the action. If the subject receives it, use *be* in the right tense plus the past participle: *is sent, was declared, has been reinforced, is being built*. Quick test: an object after the gap means active. TCAS68: "a special cleaning system that ___ after each use" → *is activated*. *Analogy:* a Shopee tracking page is all passive, because your parcel never does anything itself. *Worked examples:* "Warnings ___ to phones by cell broadcast" → *were sent*. "Since the rain stopped, barriers ___" → *have been reinforced*. *Trap:* *happen, occur, arise* are never passive.

**Passive infinitives and gerunds.** The word before the gap decides the shape. After a modal you need *be* + V3, after *to* you need *to be* + V3, and after -ing verbs and prepositions you need *being* + V3. TCAS66: "Some sites allow messages to ___ anonymously" → *be left*. TCAS69: *the next foundation to be questioned*. *Analogy:* "be" is an idol who changes outfits for each stage, while the V3 dancer wears the same costume all night. *Worked examples:* "Any event can ___ in minutes" → *be faked*. "Nobody likes ___" (then people share fakes) → *being fooled*. *Trap:* an option with the right shape but active voice, such as *to question* or *fooling*.

**Causatives.** *Have / get + thing + V3* means someone else does it for you. With a person: *have / make / let + person + base*, but *get + person + to*. TCAS68: *have it styled*. *Analogy:* frying the kaphrao yourself is "cooking dinner". Ordering it on LINE MAN is "having dinner delivered". *Worked examples:* "Pun got his brother ___ it" → *to fix*. "The photographer made everyone ___ the shot" → *retake*, and in the passive the *to* comes back: *were made to retake*. *Trap:* choosing by sound.

## Level 3 · Mood & patterns

**The subjunctive.** After *suggest, recommend, insist, demand, propose* and *It is essential that*, the verb in the that-clause is the bare base form for every subject: *that she go, that exams be tailored*. TCAS67: "It is suggested that exams ___ to specific functions" → *be tailored*. Also: *It is essential to maintain*. *Analogy:* the coach's whiteboard says "Mint, defend left", with plain verbs and no -s. *Worked examples:* "The council proposed that the school ___ a phone-free lunch" → *introduce*. "It is essential that the warnings ___ seriously" → *be taken*. *Trap:* *will be tailored* sounds natural, but *will* never follows the trigger.

**Mixed and reduced conditionals.** Each clause has its own clock. A past condition can have a present result. TCAS68: "If these traditions hadn't been passed down, we ___ about them today" → *might not even know*. Reduced: *if not prepared correctly, if necessary* (V3 when the subject receives the action). *Analogy:* a game save file, where a choice three levels ago decides what you have now. *Worked examples:* "If she hadn't written the recipes down, we ___ how to cook them today" → *wouldn't know*. "Fermented fish can be dangerous ___" → *if not stored properly*. *Trap:* ignoring *now* or *today* in the result clause.

**Verb patterns.** The verb before the gap chooses the pattern. *Avoid, risk* and *consider* take -ing. *Fail* and *manage* take *to*. *Allow, encourage, enable* and *urge* take a person + *to*. *Ban, prevent, stop* and *discourage* take a person + *from* -ing. TCAS68: *ban students from having*, not *prevent students to have*. *Analogy:* a travel adaptor, where each verb is a socket with one plug shape. *Worked examples:* "Schools risk ___ a useful tool" → *losing*. "No rule can prevent a teenager ___ a second phone" → *from bringing*. *Trap:* options that pair a real verb with another verb's pattern.

## 60-second exam tactic
Four forms of one verb? Don't read the options yet. One: underline the time anchor, even in the previous sentence. Two: bracket the subject and say head noun + gap together. Three: object after the gap? Active. Receiving subject, no object? Passive. Four: check the word before the gap — modal, *to*, -ing verb, trigger + *that*, *have / get*, *ban / allow*. Now open the options and cross out every form that breaks an answer. One usually survives in thirty seconds.

## Quick-fire self-test
1. "So far, only two schools ___ up for the trial." (signed / have signed) → **have signed**, because *so far* reaches now.
2. "The results of the survey of 2,000 students ___ surprising." (was / were) → **were**, because the head is *results*.
3. "The new rule is expected ___ next week." (to announce / to be announced) → **to be announced**.
4. "The coach insisted that every player ___ on time." (arrives / arrive) → **arrive**, the subjunctive.
5. "If I hadn't stayed up so late, I ___ so tired now." (wouldn't have been / wouldn't be) → **wouldn't be**, because *now* makes it a mixed conditional.

## Pep talk
Reading passages change every year; the verb system does not. Four questions, asked in the same order, turn a guess into a procedure. Practise them on TikTok captions, headlines and your own essays until they're automatic. By March you won't choose verbs by sound, and every verb item is 1.25 points in the bag. Satriwithaya, you've got this.
