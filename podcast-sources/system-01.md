# TCAS70 Launchpad · System 01 — Conversation Code

**Who it's for:** M5 and M6 students at Satriwithaya (and anyone else) preparing for A-Level English (82) on 14 March 2570. This episode covers Section I, items 1–20: three short conversations with four blanks each, plus one long conversation with eight blanks that always ends with a twist. That's 25 points, and it's the most learnable quarter of the paper.

## The big idea

Every blank is one turn in a conversation, and a conversation is a chain: each line answers the one before it and sets up the one after it. The examiners remove one link but leave both neighbours. So the golden rule of System 01 is: **read the line after the blank first.** It tells you the function of the missing line (question or answer? thanks or apology?), its polarity (yes or no), its tone, and the relationship between the speakers. The wrong options are always perfect English. They are wrong only because they don't fit the conversation.

## Level 1 · Everyday reactions

**Reacting to news.** Weigh the news before you react. Good news gets *Congratulations!*, bad news gets *I'm so sorry to hear that*, surprising news gets *You're kidding!*, and a worry that has disappeared gets *What a relief!* Timing matters: *Good luck* comes before an event and *Congratulations* after a success. Analogy: the reaction buttons under a chat message. Nobody sends a laughing face to "my cat is sick."
- Worked example: "Tomorrow's swimming test has been postponed." — ___ — "You've been dreading it all week." The key is *That must be a relief!* *Congratulations!* fails because a postponed test isn't an achievement.
- Worked example (TCAS68 pattern): a friend hears that a singer got married, and the next line is "I thought she was living in the US…". That's disbelief, so the answer is *You're kidding!*, not *Congratulations!*
- **Trap:** the "magnet word". *Married* or *passed* pulls you towards *Congratulations*, but the next line shows the real feeling.

**Agreeing and disagreeing.** Agreement works like a volume dial: *You can say that again* (10), *I suppose so* (5), *I'm not so sure* (2), *I'm afraid I disagree* (0). To agree with a negative sentence you say *Neither do I* or *Me neither*, never *So do I*. With negative questions, English answers the fact: "Isn't it on Saturday?" "Yes, it is" means it IS on Saturday. Thai logic leads students the other way.
- Worked example: "I don't think a total ban would work." — ___ — "Students would just hide their phones." The key is *Neither do I*; *So do I* is the polarity trap.
- Worked example: ___ "But for the whole school day? That's too much." The "But" shows partial agreement: *Fair enough, I suppose.*
- **Trap:** a following "But…" means the blank agreed only partly, or pushed back.

**Thanks and apologies.** These come in pairs, like plugs and sockets. Thanks → *You're welcome / My pleasure / Don't mention it*. Sorry → *Never mind / Don't worry about it / No harm done*. Someone tried to help but failed → *Thanks anyway*. And *I'm afraid…* has nothing to do with fear: it softens bad news.
- Worked example: "I'm afraid all three copies are out on loan." — ___ — "Wait, don't go yet!" The key is *Oh well, thanks anyway.*
- **Trap:** *Don't mention it* after an apology. It's a good plug in the wrong socket.

## Level 2 · Getting things done

**Requests, offers, permission, invitations.** First ask who is going to do the action. *Would you mind…?* works upside down, because *mind* means "be bothered by", so *No, not at all* means yes, go ahead. Accept invitations with *Count me in!* and decline with *I'd love to, but…*
- Worked example: "Would you mind giving me your order number?" — ___ — "It's FG-2047." The key is *Not at all.*

**Complaints and service recovery.** Every complaint follows a script, like a Formula 1 pit stop: polite complaint → apology → reason, "but there's no excuse" → fix → something extra (a complimentary drink, a voucher) → thanks. TCAS69 opened with exactly this script in a restaurant.
- **Trap:** a line in the wrong mouth. A waiter never says "You should have ordered earlier," and a guest never thanks you for a free breakfast before it has been offered.

**Advice and warnings.** The strength ladder runs from *How about…? / Why don't you…?* up to *You should…* and then *You'd better… or else*. Think of the GPS voice: "you could take the expressway," "turn left NOW," and the sad one, "you should have turned left." *Should have* looks back at a mistake, so it can't answer "What should I do?"
- Worked example: ___ — "Every night? That sounds exhausting, but it's worth a try." The echo word "every night" points to *Why don't you do one timed article every night?*

## Level 3 · Reading the turn

**The line after decides.** Match its shape first. *Yes, I do* means the blank was a Do-question. *About two days ago* means When. *I'm afraid not* means a hopeful yes/no question. *Why? What happened?* means the blank was a statement hinting at a problem. In TCAS66, a one-word answer, "Medium", proved the blank asked about size.
- **Trap:** right topic, wrong shape. "What is your warranty number?" can't be answered with "Yes, I do."

**Register.** Who is talking to whom? Students ask professors politely (*May I ask…? Could you possibly…?*). They never give professors orders or advice. Friends can say *Got a sec?* Staff are always polite. Teachers sometimes use dry irony: in TCAS67 a teacher who caught two students on TikTok responded to their excuse with an ironic task, looking up "inattentive" and explaining it to the class. Analogy: dress codes. Don't wear pyjama language to a professor's office.

**The twist.** The long conversation is a short story with a punchline. TCAS67 had Rome and Julie's dramatic love, which ended when Rome asked to take another girl to the party. TCAS68 had two girls on a video call who discover identical rings from the same boyfriend. TCAS69 had a professor who scolds a class for an hour and then learns he's in the wrong room. The tactic is to read the Situation and the last five lines first, track who knows what, and reject "before-twist" options that fit the romantic or confident mood of the opening but not the ending.

## 60-second exam tactic

1. Read the Situation line: where are they, and who are they?
2. For each blank, cover the options and read the line **after** it.
3. Label that line (answer, reaction, agreement, refusal) and predict the blank.
4. Match shape first (auxiliary, question word, yes/no), then topic, then tone.
5. In Part II, read the last five lines before you touch item 13.
6. If two options survive, choose the one that suits the relationship.

## Quick-fire self-test

1. "I don't like durian." How do you agree? **Neither do I / Me neither.**
2. "Would you mind closing the door?" Which answer means you'll close it: "Yes, I would" or "Not at all"? **Not at all.**
3. The reply is "Since March." What kind of question was the blank? **How long / Since when.**
4. Someone tried to help you but couldn't. What do you say? **Thanks anyway.**
5. Your friend passed her exam. "Good luck!" or "Well done!"? **Well done! Good luck comes before an event.**

## Closing pep talk

Section I isn't a vocabulary contest. It tests whether you can listen like a good friend: notice how someone feels, answer what they actually asked, and speak to a professor differently from how you speak to your best friend. You already do this every day in Thai. Now do it on paper, always looking one line ahead. Twenty items, twenty-five points. Let's go and collect them.
