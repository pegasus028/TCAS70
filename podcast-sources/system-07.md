# System 07 · Vocabulary in Context — Podcast Briefing

**For:** Thai M5–M6 students (B1–C1) at Satriwithaya and beyond, preparing for the TCAS70 A-Level English paper (subject 82) on 14 March 2027. Two hosts, warm and lively, a mix of English with the occasional Thai reassurance.

## The big idea

Every TCAS paper tests words you have never seen, and it will do the same next March: *set in stone*, *ultimate*, *intake*, *sedentary*, *vital*, *trying times*, *nefarious*, *upshot*, *attire* all came up between TCAS66 and TCAS69. The secret: you are rarely expected to *know* the word, only to *read around it*. This system gives students three tools: find the clue, test the replacement, and know the word partners that Text Completion loves.

## Level 1 — Clues

**Context clues (the four types).** Writers who use a hard word usually build a ramp to it. There are four kinds of ramp: a **definition** (*is, means, that is*, or a pair of commas), a **contrast** (*but, unlike, whereas* — the word means the opposite of the other side), an **example** (*such as, including* — what do the examples share?) and a **restatement** (a dash, *in other words*, or the next sentence repeats the idea in easy words).
*Analogy:* a detective who doesn't know the thief's name but reads the footprints around the window.
*Worked examples:* "Unlike her outspoken sister, Nan is rather **reticent**" — contrast, so reticent means reserved. "The shelter asked for **non-perishable** food, such as rice, canned fish and dried noodles" — example; they all keep for months, so non-perishable means long-lasting.
*The trap:* reading a contrast clue the wrong way round. In TCAS66, *set in stone* was followed by "whereas … a more flexible view", so it meant *unchangeable*, the opposite of flexible.

**Closest in meaning: replace and reread.** Cover the options, predict a simple meaning, then put each option into the sentence and reread the whole line. The key keeps the grammar, the message and the strength.
*Analogy:* a substitute member in a K-pop line-up has to fit the choreography and sing the same part in the same key.
*Worked examples:* "Swimming could **alleviate** the pain, although it would not cure it" — *ease* passes; *remove* is too strong. "Interest **diminished** after the first month… the app still had a small group of loyal users" — *lessened* passes; *disappeared* is too strong; *grew* fits the grammar but flips the message.
*The trap:* answering from your favourite dictionary meaning without rereading. Three distractor types to name out loud: another sense of the word, right field but wrong strength, and fits-but-changes-the-message.

**Familiar words, exam meanings.** When TCAS tests an *easy* word, get suspicious. *Trying times* = difficult times. *Thanks to* = because of, even for bad news. *Intake* = the amount you eat or drink. *Address* a problem = deal with it. *Sound* advice = reliable.
*Analogy:* your friend Krit is a footballer, but on Saturday mornings he works at his aunt's noodle shop. Easy words have second jobs.
*Worked examples:* "**Thanks to** a burst pipe, the exam was moved" — *because of*, not *grateful for*. "The principal **summoned** the two boys to her office" — *ordered to come*; *invited* is too weak.
*The trap:* the everyday meaning is always an option. Cross it out first.

## Level 2 — Academic words

**Academic verbs.** Most of them tell you a direction. Better or stronger: *enhance, foster, reinforce, bolster*. Worse or weaker: *undermine, exacerbate, erode*. Slower or limited: *impede, hinder, hamper, curb*. Less severe: *mitigate, alleviate*.
*Analogy:* the BTS control room — every verb is a button. Which button did the cause just press?
*Worked examples:* "The floods could not be prevented, but the city worked to **mitigate** their effects" — *reduce*, not *prevent*. "Scrolling can **exacerbate** the sleep problems many teenagers already have" — *make worse*, not *cause*, because the problem already exists.
*The trap:* right direction, wrong timing. Check whether the problem was already there. Also watch partners: *attribute X to Y* means Y caused X.

**Evaluative adjectives and word parts.** These carry the writer's judgement and its strength. Decode with word parts: *un-, in-, dis-* = not; *mis-* = wrongly; *bene-* = good; *mal-* = bad; *sede-* = sit, so *sedentary* = involving lots of sitting.
*Analogy:* a volume knob. *Common* is volume 4; *ubiquitous* is volume 10.
*Worked examples:* "QR payment is **ubiquitous** — almost every vendor has a code" — *found everywhere*, not just *popular*. "The open day drew an **unprecedented** 40,000 visitors, more than double the previous record" — *never seen before*, not *unexpected*.
*The trap:* the right family at the wrong volume.

**Abstract nouns.** They are labels for ideas. Results: *upshot, outcome, repercussion*. Plus and minus: *drawback, trade-off, incentive*. People: *proponent, critic, sceptic*. Checking and agreeing: *scrutiny, consensus*. Qualities: *integrity, propensity*.
*Analogy:* folder names on your laptop.
*Worked examples:* "The **upshot** was that plastic waste fell by only 8%, far less than the 40% it had hoped for" — *outcome*, not *target*. "The university came under intense **scrutiny**" — close examination; the partner *came under* is the giveaway.
*The trap:* a distractor from the same story doing a different job — goal, cause or result? Ask before you look.

## Level 3 — Word partners

**Collocations.** English pairs words by habit, not logic: *play a role, pose a risk, raise concerns, take X into account, reach a consensus, draw a conclusion*. The noun chooses its verb.
*Analogy:* mango and sticky rice. Mango with fried rice is not illegal, but everyone stares.
*Worked examples:* "Sleep ___ a key role" — *plays*. "Heat can ___ a serious risk" — *pose*.
*The trap:* translating "ทำ" into *do* or *make*. Say the whole pair aloud instead.

**Phrasal verbs.** Verb plus particle equals a new word. Learn a formal twin: *carry out* = conduct, *look into* = investigate, *cut down on* = reduce, *come down with* = catch an illness, *bring out* = reveal.
*Analogy:* emoji combos — one extra emoji flips the message.
*Worked examples:* "Many dengue cases ___ standing water" — *stem from* (looking back to the cause), not *lead to*. "Krit ___ a high fever" — *came down with*, not *came up with*.
*The trap:* same verb, different particle, and cause–effect verbs that point the wrong way.

**Dependent prepositions.** The word before the gap owns the preposition: *focus on, concerned about, benefit from, prone to, ban X from -ing, seen as*. After a preposition, use a noun or -ing: *contribute to reducing*.
*Analogy:* phone and charger — each port takes one cable.
*Worked examples:* "Parents are concerned ___ screen time" — *about*. "Bans may result ___ teenagers hiding what they do" — *in*, because the hiding is the effect; *result from* would point back to a cause.
*The trap:* meaning-shifters — *result in/from*, *concerned about/with*, *seen as* (never *seen like*).

## 60-second exam tactic

1. Box the word. Read one sentence before and one after.
2. Name the clue: definition, contrast, example or restatement.
3. Cover the options and write your own simple meaning.
4. Drop each option in and reread the full sentence.
5. Kill the three distractor types: other sense, wrong strength, changed message.
6. In a cloze gap, find the head word — noun for collocations, word before the gap for prepositions — and say the pair aloud.

## Quick-fire self-test

1. "Unlike the old rule, which was lenient, the new one punishes every late arrival." Clue type? — **Contrast** (lenient = not strict).
2. "Thanks to the traffic, we missed the start." Replace *thanks to*. — **Because of.**
3. Heat can ___ a serious risk to children: *make / pose / do / have*? — **pose.**
4. "Scrolling can exacerbate existing sleep problems." Why not *cause*? — **The problems already exist; exacerbate = make worse.**
5. Teenagers are more prone ___ accidents when tired. — **to.**

## Closing pep talk

TCAS will put a word in front of you that you have never seen. That is fine — it is supposed to happen. You don't need a bigger dictionary in your head; you need a calmer pair of eyes. Read around the word, predict, replace, reread. Every clue you notice is a free point. Go and collect them.
