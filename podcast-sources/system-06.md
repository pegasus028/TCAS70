# System 06 · Visuals & Numbers — Podcast Briefing

**For:** M5–M6 students at Satriwithaya preparing for the TCAS70 A-Level English paper (subject 82), March 2027. This episode covers Reading Part IV, Visuals (items 39–44): two visuals, three questions each. That's six items and 7.5 points, and it's the most predictable section of the paper.

## The big idea

Visual questions are not maths questions and not really reading questions. They are **frame questions**. Every table, chart and diagram has a frame: a title (what, where, when), units, headings and a small note. Read the frame first and most of the answers are already decided. Skip it and the examiner's traps are waiting. The golden rule: **title, units, notes, then numbers.**

## Level 1 · Tables

**Reading tables and rankings.** Read the title and headings before any number. Then, for each option, write its number next to it. TCAS69 asked, "Of the four provinces below, which one had the lowest number of foreign tourists?" The trap is that "lowest of these four" is not the bottom of the table. *Analogy:* a table is like the BTS map. Check the line and the direction before you look for your station.
- Example 1: Of Krabi (904,000), Songkhla (452,000), Phang Nga (297,000) and Prachuap Khiri Khan (318,000), which is second-highest? Songkhla.
- Example 2: Is Prachuap Khiri Khan "fewer than 300,000"? No: 318,000. Boundary numbers are placed in options on purpose.
*Trap:* ranking the whole table instead of the four options.

**Arithmetic.** Turn words into one operation. "Times as many" means divide. "Combined" means add. "Difference" and "how many more" mean subtract. "Closest pair" means the smallest gap. For "approximately", round first and find a target. *Analogy:* a 7-Eleven receipt, where the only skill is pressing the right button.
- Example 1: Bangkok 5,436,000 is approximately five times as many as which province? 5.4 million ÷ 5 ≈ 1.1 million, so Chiang Mai (1,086,000). Krabi gives about six times, which makes it the near miss.
- Example 2: How many more visitors did Bangkok get than Phuket and Chonburi combined? Add first (4,617,000), then subtract: 819,000. The wrong options are the answers you get if you stop halfway.
*Trap:* percent vs percentage points. A fall from 40% to 29% is 11 percentage points, not "11 percent".

**What the visual shows and the best title.** A correct answer names what is measured, for whom and when. Wrong answers change the measure (visitors become income), change the time, add a cause ("because of phones") or shrink to one row. *Analogy:* checking a concert ticket at the gate: artist, date, zone.
- Example 1: A sleep chart in hours per grade is not about "how many students sleep eight hours".
- Example 2: In a table of "% of students in each grade", the 38 in the M5 row means 38% of M5 students, not 38 people.
*Trap:* the "interesting cause". No chart of averages can tell you why.

## Level 2 · Charts

**Trend language.** Direction plus size plus span. Surge and soar mean a big, fast rise. Plummet and plunge mean a big, fast fall. Level off means stop changing. Fluctuate means up and down. Peak is the top. Steadily describes a regular pace; slightly describes a small size. *Analogy:* rides at a theme park, from the rollercoaster launch to the big drop to the flat platform.
- Example 1: Short-video time surged from 2019 to 2021 (35 to 86 minutes) but levelled off from 2022 to 2026 (102 to 109). Same line, different span.
- Example 2: Online games peaked in 2020 and then fell steadily, not slightly, because they almost halved.
*Trap:* a description that is true of another span of the same line.

**Pie charts.** Every slice is a share of the whole sample. Combine slices by adding them, and turn percentages into people: 34% of 2,000 = 680. The hard question comes from the note under the chart. TCAS69 asked for the difference between women and men within one group "among the total sample". Ask "percent of what?" If the sub-numbers add up to the slice (14 + 8 = 22), they are shares of the whole, so the gap is 14 − 8 = 6 points. *Analogy:* a pizza, where the note describes the toppings on one slice. They are not new slices.
*Trap:* subtracting from the slice (22 − 14) or picking one sub-number.

**Comparing groups.** Make a gap list: for every category, write the difference and which group is higher. TCAS68 asked for the largest female–male difference in teen spending. The tallest bars are not the biggest gap. *Analogy:* a badminton score, where 11–4 is a thrashing but 21–18 is a close game.
- Example 1: Games & apps (19% vs 4%, gap 15) beats Food & drinks (34% vs 31%, gap 3).
- Example 2: "More than twice"? Clothes are 22 vs 11, which is exactly twice, so the answer is no.
*Trap:* judging by bar height, and forgetting that "% of each group's spending" is not baht.

## Level 3 · Diagrams

**Flowcharts.** Every diamond is an if-clause. Start at the box the stem gives you, follow only its branch, and move one arrow for "next". In TCAS68's smoking flowchart, the first step was asking whether the patient wanted to quit. *Analogy:* a Grab route with turns at each junction.
- Example: If a student with heat illness cannot answer questions, do NOT give a drink. That same action is correct on the other branch.
*Trap:* a real box from the wrong branch.

**Infographics.** Read the headings first (sources, pathways, where it ends up, effects) and answer from the heading the stem names. The best title is the topic plus the headings, like TCAS67's "PM 2.5: Sources, Components, and Effects". *Analogy:* 7-Eleven shelves, where something can be in the shop but not in the fridge.
- Example: Farm soil is in the microplastics infographic, but as an end point, not a source.
*Trap:* "It's in the picture, so it can't be the NOT answer."

**NOT provided and small print.** For each option, point to the evidence. If you can't point, it's not provided. TCAS66's key was "When germs are born", because nothing in the diagram mentioned time. Read asterisks: a 2026 bar marked "January–June only" can't be compared with a full year. Amount is not rate. When a survey allowed multiple responses, the percentages can't be added and the overlap is unknown. *Analogy:* the small print on a snack packet.
*Trap:* believing the dramatic bar before reading the footnote.

## 60-second exam tactic

1. Ten seconds on the frame: title, units, headings, notes, asterisks.
2. Circle the key word in the stem: second-highest, combined, approximately, next, NOT.
3. Write the numbers next to the options, and write every calculation down.
4. For NOT/EXCEPT, mark each option ✓ (shown), ✗ (contradicted) or ? (not shown).
5. Keep "approximately" honest: round, find the target, and choose the closest option.

## Quick-fire self-test

1. China 3,120,000 is approximately eight times as many as which country: Laos 345,000 or Germany 390,000? **Germany (3,120,000 ÷ 8 = 390,000).**
2. 22% of a pie is split into 14% female and 8% male (of the whole). What is the gap? **6 percentage points.**
3. A line goes 102, 106, 108, 109, 109. Surged or levelled off? **Levelled off.**
4. A chart in thousand tonnes shows 64. How much is that? **64,000 tonnes.**
5. Week 4 had 118 kg of food scraps in three school days, and week 2 had 196 kg in five. Which week had more per day? **About the same (39.3 vs 39.2 kg).**

## Pep talk

Part IV is where careful students collect easy points. The examiners aren't testing whether you're good at maths. They're testing whether you slow down for ten seconds and read the frame. Read the frame, write the numbers, and these six items are yours.
